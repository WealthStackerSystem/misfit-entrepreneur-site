Episode 477 · The Misfit Entrepreneur

Garrett Gunderson on the 4 Areas Entrepreneurs Leak Money and the Coordinated Team Strategy the Ultra-Wealthy Use

with Garrett Gunderson

Apple Podcasts Spotify YouTube
The 60-Second Version
  • Garrett built and failed at the family office model twice before AI and long-term relationships with a CPA and attorney made it viable at scale for entrepreneurs earning $350K to $10M.
  • The four biggest areas entrepreneurs leak money are taxes, interest rates, hidden investment fees, and poor insurance structure—fixing these beats doubling investment returns.
  • The ultra-wealthy are not smarter; they use a coordinated team of specialists whose strategies align, instead of leaving the business owner as an uninformed middle manager.
  • Velocity of money matters more than accumulation—every idle dollar loses value, and investing back into your own business typically outperforms diversifying into assets you don't control.
  • Garrett's three pieces of final wisdom: give love without expecting reciprocation, design a process worth playing in itself, and recognize that the greatest wealth is found in relationships.
About the Guest

Garrett Gunderson

Garrett Gunderson is a New York Times and Wall Street Journal bestselling author of 10 books, including the number one bestseller Killing Sacred Cows. He grew up in a coal mining town in Utah and went on to build an eight-figure financial firm that made the Inc. 500. A financial advocate for entrepreneurs, Garrett has personally interviewed 23 billionaires and spent his career teaching business owners how to make, keep, and grow wealth without deferring life. He is the founder of Multiplier Family Office.

The Conversation

What We Covered

01 · The Origin Story
A lot of people may be hearing your story for the first time. How did you get to where you are?
  • Garrett grew up in a coal mining town in Utah where his father, grandfathers, and great-grandfather all worked the mines. His great-grandfather left Italy in 1913 and survived a mine disaster by becoming a goat herder, never meeting his own daughter until she was a few years old.
  • His father refused to let him follow the same path, so Garrett started washing and detailing cars at 15. He won a state business competition at 17, taking home $5,000, and immediately tried to invest it — his mother wouldn't sign as custodian.
  • At 18 he made his first investment, a bad one at $70 a month, which pulled him into the financial industry. When markets fell in 2000 he started questioning whether mutual funds and life insurance were actually how wealthy people built wealth — and started getting access to closed-door meetings with people worth $30 million who had only 5% in stocks.
  • At 21 he co-founded a financial firm with two partners in their 30s, focused on understanding how money really worked for the wealthy — eventually dropping product licenses, partnering with attorneys and CPAs, and shifting toward helping businesses create cash flow rather than extracting it.
02 · The Breakthrough
You've been open about trying to build Multiplier Family Office twice before and failing. Why didn't it work those first two times, and what finally made it work?
  • The first attempt failed because Garrett tried to own every service in-house — buying a property and casualty company, starting a boutique mortgage company — which created too many moving pieces. When he referred work out instead, he lost visibility and coordination broke down, leaving clients as their own middle managers between attorneys and CPAs who didn't agree with each other.
  • A compounding crisis hit: his two business partners died in a plane crash in June 2006, and then 2008 arrived. Over-leveraged in 100-plus properties he never actually enjoyed, Garrett spent a year in survival mode with two young kids and a marriage under severe strain. He got through without declaring bankruptcy but called it one of the most painful periods of his life.
  • The second attempt hit a hard ceiling at 95 to 100 clients. The model that actually worked — a hands-on, personalized program — capped out because attorneys and accountants ran out of capacity, especially at tax season, killing the proactivity that made the program worth having. He also sunk too much time and money into bloated technology that never delivered. He eventually sold the company.
  • The breakthrough came when AI and technology matured enough to change the unit economics entirely. Work that used to take 20 to 30 hours to build for a client can now be done in 20 to 30 minutes. The CPA and attorney he brought in are the same people he went to college with and worked with for over 20 years — trusted partners rather than hired vendors — which solved the coordination problem that killed the first two versions.
  • The result: 180 clients, no capacity constraint, one refund request in two years, and a price range of $500 to $5,000 a month for services that previously cost $3,000 a month for tax planning alone.
03 · The Growth Engine
Now that it's working, what does the actual delivery model look like — and how are you able to serve a market that nobody else is really serving?
  • The target client is a business owner earning between $350,000 and $10 million — a range Garrett describes as almost entirely unserved, since traditional family offices require a $30 million minimum and solo CPAs or attorneys work in silos without coordination.
  • Clients upload their documents and a purpose-built AI system analyzes them in real time, trained on a proprietary "business brain" that learns from every new strategy applied across the client base. When something new applies, the system flags the other clients it could benefit — proactive rather than reactive.
  • The attorney reviews pre-analyzed, redlined files in minutes rather than hours, saving the $600-per-hour clock for actual judgment calls. An AI bot handles routine legal education so the attorney's time goes only where it can't be replaced. One attorney can now review ten files in the time it used to take to review one.
  • The firm draws a hard line at $10 million in revenue. Above that, the complexity requires one dedicated person per client at a level Garrett says other firms already handle well. Referring those clients out keeps Multiplier from recreating the constraints that killed the earlier versions.
  • Tax strategy begins in July for the current year rather than October or December, which Garrett treats as a core competitive differentiator — the difference between capturing available deductions and losing them because planning started too late.
04 · The Filter
What are the things you've decided not to pursue — markets, expansions, opportunities — and how do you make those calls?
  • Garrett hard-caps the client base at businesses under $10 million in revenue. Above that threshold, complexity outpaces what the current model can handle without degrading results, so those clients are referred to firms built for that scale.
  • He will not own the service companies again. The first failure came directly from trying to control property and casualty, mortgage, and financial planning under one roof. The current model relies on deeply trusted long-term partners rather than owned subsidiaries.
  • He walked away from a seven-figure licensing deal tied to his previous company after selling it, accepting the income loss rather than continuing a relationship where he couldn't control quality or direction.
  • During the years between his second failed attempt and Multiplier, Garrett deliberately did not rush back into finance. He pursued comedy, produced a special that reached Amazon Prime, did two tours, and only returned to financial services after sitting with the question of what he actually wanted to build — a pause he describes as the hardest thing to do but necessary to avoid repeating earlier mistakes.
  • He avoids asset classes he doesn't understand or can't influence, and counsels clients against the same. His position is that diversifying into things outside your investor DNA — real estate you don't understand, stocks you have no influence over — creates losses without lessons, which is a worse outcome than a business mistake you can learn from and correct.
05 · The People
You've built this around a core team rather than a staff of dozens. Who are the people inside Multiplier, and why does the client experience depend on them specifically?
  • The CPA and the attorney at the center of Multiplier are not hired vendors — they are people Garrett went to college with and has worked alongside for over 20 years. That history eliminates the coordination breakdown that destroyed the earlier versions, where outside attorneys and CPAs would contradict each other and leave clients as their own middle managers.
  • The attorney saw the writing on the wall for his profession and chose to get ahead of it. He trains the entire Multiplier team for an hour at a time on fundamentals and flags to watch for — which means he reserves his $600-per-hour judgment for decisions that actually require it, rather than spending that time on document prep that AI now handles.
  • Each client-facing team member operates as a quarterback, with one person able to serve 50 to 60 clients because the technology absorbs the administrative burden — gathering documents, filling out questionnaires, pre-analyzing and redlining files — before any human looks at the work.
  • What clients experience is proactivity rather than reaction. Tax strategy starts in July for the current year. The system flags relevant new strategies across the client base automatically. The people on the team are not waiting to be asked — the model is structurally designed so that waiting is not an option.
  • Garrett treats the near-zero attrition — one refund request in two years across 180 clients — as a direct result of who is in the room, not just what the technology does. Trusted long-term partners who share his philosophy on how money actually works for entrepreneurs are the reason the quality holds.
06 · What Broke
You hit a hard ceiling at around 95 to 100 clients in your second attempt. What specifically broke, and what did you learn from it?
  • The thing that broke was proactivity. The program that worked best was the most hands-on one, and when attorneys and CPAs hit capacity — especially at tax season — the team shifted from getting ahead of problems to just keeping up. The defining feature of the service became the first casualty of growth.
  • Alongside the capacity problem, Garrett sunk significant time and money into building proprietary technology that never delivered. He held on to it past the point of reason — a classic sunk-cost trap — before finally accepting the loss and moving on.
  • The three-tier program structure he built as a workaround created a different problem: the lower tiers got results he wasn't proud of, but clients stayed because the price was low. He describes the 200-dollar-a-month tier as hurting his soul because people could say they were in his program without actually getting meaningful help.
  • What replaced it was a structural inversion: instead of building capacity limits into the model and hoping technology would catch up, he waited until AI had actually matured enough to change the unit economics — 20 to 30 hours of client prep compressed to 20 to 30 minutes — before relaunching.
  • The lesson he took was that a great program at 100 clients that degrades at 101 is not a scalable business. The only fix is building the constraint out of the model before you scale, not patching it after the cracks appear.
07 · Worth Knowing
Why has no one built a coordinated financial service for business owners between $350,000 and $10 million in revenue before now?
  • Traditional family offices require a $30 million minimum. Solo CPAs and attorneys work in silos and rarely coordinate. The entire market segment between $350,000 and $10 million in business revenue has been structurally unserved — not because demand was absent, but because the unit economics made it impossible to staff profitably.
  • The core problem was labor cost. A coordinated service requires an attorney reviewing documents, a CPA doing proactive strategy, and a financial advisor quarterbacking the relationship. At $600 an hour for the attorney alone, the hours required to build a client file made the math work only at price points most small business owners won't pay.
  • AI broke the labor cost constraint directly. Document analysis, questionnaire completion, file redlining, and routine legal education — the work that consumed most of the professional hours — can now be handled before any licensed professional touches the file. The attorney's time is now spent only where human judgment is irreplaceable.
  • The second structural barrier was coordination. Even firms that tried to serve this market by referring to outside specialists lost visibility the moment the referral left the building. Clients became translators between advisors who disagreed with each other, and the value of coordination disappeared. Garrett's solution was to bring the CPA and attorney in-house as partners, not contractors, with shared access to the same client data in real time.
  • The result is a price point — $500 to $5,000 a month — for a fully coordinated service that previously cost $3,000 a month for tax planning alone, if you could find someone willing to do it. The category was empty not because no one wanted to fill it, but because the technology to make it economically viable did not exist until now.

The Misfit 3™

Garrett's Three

The three things Garrett would leave behind for the generations that come after.

01

Give love without limits

The more love you give, the more love you can receive, and there is no limitation. Most people hold back because they fear they won't get reciprocation. Show up that way consistently and you'll attract the right people long term.

02

Design a game worth playing

Design a game worth playing and you've already won. The journey itself is the win. Don't get addicted only to outcomes.

03

Relationships are the biggest riches

The biggest riches in the world are in relationships. If you have great relationships, life is worth it. No amount of money can compensate for loneliness.

Put It To Work

Five Things You Can Act On

  1. Stop being the middle manager of your own finances.When you refer your CPA to your attorney without coordination, you become the go-between and things fall through the cracks. A coordinated team catches things you never would alone. Who is actually quarterbacking your financial house right now?
  2. Plug the four leaks before you invest another dollar.Tax, interest, investment fees, and insurance structure are the four places most entrepreneurs silently bleed money. Fix those first and you create cash flow today, not in 30 years. Which of the four is costing you the most right now?
  3. Reinvest in your business before funding a retirement account.Your business is your highest-return asset and you have actual influence over the outcome. Starving it to max out a plan you can't touch for decades slows your real wealth engine. Are you funding the vehicle with the most upside or the one Wall Street tells you to?
  4. Replace retirement with recurring revenue.When your recurring revenue covers your lifestyle, you are work-optional. That is financial freedom, not a number sitting in an account you are afraid to touch. What would it take to build one new recurring revenue stream in the next 90 days?
  5. Address what is actually happening and course-correct fast.Ignoring a problem on stage or in business costs you everything that comes after it. Name the issue, own your part, and adjust before it compounds. What is one problem in your business right now that you have been avoiding instead of addressing?
