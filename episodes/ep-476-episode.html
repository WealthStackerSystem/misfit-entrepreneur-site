The 60-Second Version
  • Nigel and his co-founder left ad tech to pursue a mission of reducing car crashes, initially building self-driving hardware before discovering a bigger opportunity in the fragmented driving school industry.
  • After finding little interest from school owners in buying their scheduling software, they became their own first customer and built Coastline Academy by operating the model themselves.
  • Coastline's acquisition strategy targets well-run, culture-strong schools in new states, using existing licenses as a regulatory shortcut while preserving the staff and community trust that make those schools worth buying.
  • AI has accelerated Coastline's engineering deployment speed and replaced traditional FP&A functions, while the core product—a calm, patient instructor in a car with a nervous teen—remains irreducibly human.
  • Nigel's biggest operational lesson is that every time a company doubles in size, roughly half its systems break, and fast-growing companies should invest proportionally rather than building for a ten-year horizon.
About the Guest

Nigel Tunacliffe

Nigel Tunacliffe is the co-founder and CEO of Coastline Academy, the largest driving school in the United States and the sixth fastest-growing education company in America according to Inc. magazine. After starting his career in ad tech, Nigel pivoted to building a mission-driven company aimed at eradicating car crashes. He grew Coastline from a scrappy California startup into a tech-enabled operation teaching over 250,000 students across 500-plus cities in nine states, largely through strategic acquisition of independent driving schools.

The Conversation

What We Covered

01 · The Origin Story
I get the sense you didn't start out to build a driving school empire. How did you actually get here?
  • Nigel and his co-founder were working at an ad tech company doing technically interesting work that had no meaningful impact on the world, and that bothered them enough to quit.
  • They started with the mission first — eradicate car crashes — and worked backward to find a business, which is the opposite of how most companies start.
  • Their first company was a hardware product designed to add modern safety features to older cars, targeted at families with new teenage drivers. It never found a market.
  • Rather than abandon the mission, they went back through hundreds of user interviews they had already conducted and looked for a different angle in the same problem space.
  • That research surfaced driving education: a fragmented industry with no national player, almost no technology, and thousands of small owner-operated schools that had never been consolidated.
  • Their original plan was to sell software to those school owners — until the owners made clear they had no interest in changing how they operated.
02 · The Breakthrough
So you built software that the market didn't want to buy. How did you go from that dead end to actually having a business?
  • The core technical insight was a scheduling algorithm that matches students to instructors across a wide geographic area and surfaces only the most logistically efficient lesson times — solving a complexity problem that made driving schools impossible to scale past a single location.
  • When driving school owners showed no interest in buying the software, Nigel and his co-founder asked a different question: what if they just became the customer of their own software and ran a school themselves?
  • They put up a basic website for Coastline Academy and turned on Google Ads before they had a license, instructors, or cars — purely to test whether demand existed.
  • Within an hour of the ads going live, people were on chat asking about lessons. The market showed itself faster than they expected.
  • That test settled the question: stop trying to sell software to a fragmented industry that doesn't want it, and instead use the software as the operating backbone of their own school.
03 · The Growth Engine
Walk us through how you scaled after that — the organic growth, then the acquisition strategy, and how you market across hundreds of cities today.
  • For the first five years Coastline grew organically: no offices, just hiring and licensing instructors market by market and using the scheduling software to match them with students.
  • Expanding outside California exposed a hard constraint — state licensing processes were slow enough to become a meaningful bottleneck on growth speed.
  • Acquiring existing schools solved the licensing problem, because Coastline could take on a school's existing state license and build from there rather than starting the approval process from scratch.
  • The acquisition pipeline turned out to be deep: thousands of owners who had run their schools for 30 years, whose kids had no interest in the business, and who were ready to retire with no obvious exit.
  • Marketing runs almost entirely on Google Ads, but the spend is tuned by a scoring system that rates every operating location by its supply-to-demand ratio — ads are only amplified where excess instructor capacity actually exists, so the company isn't selling lessons it can't fulfill.
  • AI has accelerated two things most: engineering pace, where the team shifted from writing code to defining requirements and reviewing pull requests, and business intelligence, where any leader can query Claude directly against underlying operational data instead of waiting on a dedicated analyst.
04 · The Filter
What does a good acquisition target actually look like to you, and where do you draw hard lines on what Coastline will and won't pursue?
  • Coastline does not do turnarounds. The non-negotiable filter is whether the instructors are happy in their work and whether students are leaving good reviews — if those two things are true, everything else is fixable.
  • Poor accounting, outdated software, older cars — none of that disqualifies a deal. What disqualifies a deal is a team that isn't engaged or customers who aren't satisfied, because those problems don't go away when the brand changes.
  • When sellers ask what will happen to their staff, Nigel's answer is consistent: the people are the reason Coastline wants to do the deal at all.
  • On adjacent markets — insurance, commercial driving, flight schools — Nigel has explored them and treats them as a distraction. His stated principle is that once you have product-market fit, anything outside of accelerating that fit is a detractor, not an opportunity.
  • He also won't over-engineer systems for longevity, because in a fast-growing company no system survives more than a few doublings intact — overinvesting in something you'll have to discard in two years is a known trap he actively avoids.
05 · The People
You said this is fundamentally a people business. What does that actually mean for how you hire, how you run acquisitions, and what a student experiences?
  • Coastline runs on three core values — people first, transparency, and efficiency — in that order, because the quality of every lesson is entirely determined by the instructor sitting in the car.
  • When evaluating an acquisition, happy instructors and good student reviews are the non-negotiables. Everything else — old cars, paper scheduling, thin accounting — is fixable. Culture is not.
  • Owners who ask what will happen to their staff after a sale get a consistent answer: the people are the reason Coastline wants to do the deal at all.
  • The actual differentiator in the lesson itself is hard to put on a feature list: instructors who are calm, patient, and actively building a nervous teenager's confidence in a skill they are learning from zero for the first time.
  • Picking students up at their home removes a friction point that most schools ignore, and an easy booking system that surfaces only logistically efficient times means the experience starts well before anyone gets in a car.
  • Because families typically use a driving school only once, the only way to build a brand is to earn a referral — which means every single lesson has to be good enough that the parent tells the next family about it.
06 · What Broke
With growth this fast across multiple states, what's a specific system that worked and then fell apart on you?
  • The original fleet model had every instructor take a Coastline car home and keep it in their driveway — no parking costs, instructors loved it, and the scheduling software handled the logistics cleanly.
  • The system broke when management grew beyond Nigel's direct span of control. Once a layer of managers existed between him and the instructors, the distributed-car model created too many situations that couldn't be solved hands-on.
  • The replacement was a hub model: physical parking locations holding two to ten cars each, where instructors check out a vehicle at the start of their day. Operationally more controllable, but harder to introduce because instructors were attached to the old arrangement.
  • The broader lesson Nigel draws is that every time the company doubles in size, roughly half its systems break — and it's genuinely hard to predict which half until it happens.
  • That pattern runs from the very first doubling (one instructor to two) all the way to hundreds, and it argues against overengineering any system for longevity, because in a fast-growing company no system survives more than a few doublings intact.
07 · Worth Knowing
You had no national competitor to study and no existing playbook. How did that shape what you had to figure out from scratch?
  • There has never been a national driving school before Coastline, which means no operational model existed to borrow from or improve on — every process for managing instructors, fleets, and multi-state licensing had to be invented.
  • The absence of a playbook had a direct effect on growth speed: when Coastline became the largest driving school in California in roughly its third year, there was no comparable operation anywhere to consult on what breaks next at that scale.
  • State licensing requirements created a structural ceiling on organic growth that a fragmented industry had never needed to solve, because no prior school had tried to expand nationally — the licensing bottleneck only became visible because Coastline was the first to hit it.
  • The acquisition strategy was not conceived as a growth lever from the start; it emerged as the practical answer to a problem — slow state approvals — that only surfaced once Coastline tried to move beyond California.
  • The same dynamic applies to fleet management, instructor supervision, and supply-demand marketing: each system had to be designed for a scale that the driving school industry had simply never reached, with no prior art to reference.

Put It To Work

Five Things You Can Act On

  1. Look for opportunities in fragmented, overlooked industries.When everyone else ignores a market, that's often where the real opportunity hides. Coastline found a massive, untapped gap in driving education that nobody had consolidated nationally. What boring or sleepy industry in your world is ripe for the same treatment?
  2. Test demand before you build the product.Coastline ran Google Ads and talked to customers before they had a single license, instructor, or car. Within an hour they had people on chat asking for lessons. Are you validating real demand before you invest time and money building something nobody wants?
  3. Acquire culture first, fix everything else later.When evaluating an acquisition, Nigel looks for happy instructors and good reviews above all else. Older cars and messy books can be fixed. A broken culture can't. Before your next acquisition or key hire, ask yourself: are the people here ones you'd choose from scratch?
  4. Remove friction at every step of your customer experience.Picking students up at home and offering easy online booking sounds small, but it's a real differentiator in a market where competitors make you come to them. The less your customer has to think or do, the more they trust you. Where is your process making customers work harder than they should?
  5. Plan for your systems to break when you double in size.Every time Coastline doubled, roughly half their systems stopped working — and it was hard to predict which half. Don't overinvest in any one system expecting it to last a decade in a fast-growing company. What systems in your business are one growth stage away from breaking?
