85 Bestsellers, Marji Ross on How to Turn Your Book Into Real Authority and Revenue

with Marji Ross — Founder, Marji Ross Consulting

Apple Podcasts Spotify YouTube
The 60-Second Version
  • Writing the book is only half the work—the harder half is marketing and selling it, which requires treating publishing like direct marketing.
  • Before writing a word, define what success looks like for you and identify the specific audience whose problem your book solves—'everyone' is never an acceptable answer.
  • To get media coverage that actually sells books, pitch the idea inside the book and tie it to something timely, not the book itself.
  • AI is useful for organizing material and mining your manuscript for quotes and social content, but it fabricates research citations and has a recognizable writing pattern that editors can spot.
  • When something in your business works, keep doing it—consistency, leverage, and gratitude are the three principles Marji credits most for long-term success.
About the Guest

Marji Ross

Marji Ross spent nearly two decades as president and publisher of Regnery Publishing, becoming the first person outside the founding family to hold that role. Under her leadership the company doubled in sales and placed more than 85 books on national bestseller lists, including 14 that reached number one. Before publishing she built a foundation in direct marketing and newsletters. She now runs Marji Ross Consulting, helping thought leaders and entrepreneurs use books to build their brands and grow their businesses.

The Conversation

What We Covered

01 · The Origin Story
You didn't start out in publishing or anything like that. Take a few minutes and tell us your story — how did you get to where you are today?
  • Marji's first meaningful job was in the newsletter industry — a world she didn't know existed until she stumbled into it. It taught her direct marketing, personal finance, and celebrity management all at once.
  • From newsletters she moved into publishing and journalism, building a skill set that was rare in traditional book publishing: she knew not just how to make a book, but how to sell one.
  • She became the first person outside the Regnery family to serve as president and publisher of Regnery Publishing, a conservative political press founded in 1947.
  • She credits much of her career trajectory to timing — being in the right market at the right moment — and treats that honestly as a factor rather than hiding it behind pure skill.
  • Her entire publishing philosophy was shaped by direct marketing principles that most book publishers never thought to apply — know your audience, know their problem, test and measure everything.
02 · The Breakthrough
After 85 books on the bestseller list and 14 at number one, you start seeing real patterns. What actually separates a book that becomes a bestseller from one that quietly disappears?
  • The core insight was that finishing a book means you are only halfway done. The hard part begins when you start figuring out how to market and sell it.
  • Regnery published nonfiction tied to current events and politics, which gave every book a built-in answer to the question every producer and gatekeeper asks: why do my viewers care about this right now?
  • Marji's three-part filter for a viable book: it has to be interesting, it has to be unique, and it has to be timely. All three, not just one.
  • The biggest mistake she saw from authors pitching her was describing their book as being for everyone — which she treated as a signal they hadn't actually figured out who it was for.
  • She required authors to answer not just 'what problem does this solve' but 'is this a problem the reader knows they have?' — because many books solve problems people don't recognize they're experiencing.
  • She pushed authors to frame problems in emotional, felt terms rather than solution-first language — a reader doesn't want a sales funnel, they want more customers.
03 · The Growth Engine
When you stepped into the role at Regnery and ended up doubling the company's sales, what did you see that needed to change, and what were the entrepreneurial lessons from actually doing it?
  • She entered at a moment when the conservative political book market was expanding rapidly and moved quickly to capture that demand rather than diversify away from it.
  • Her primary rule: when something works, keep doing it. She saw too many publishers abandon successful formulas out of boredom or the desire to do something new.
  • She applied a direct marketing discipline to testing — always defining in advance what decision the test results would drive, not just collecting data for its own sake.
  • She refused to publish 'a little of everything' the way most small publishers did, instead focusing exclusively on one genre and becoming the recognized leader in it.
  • Every new initiative had to meet a leverage standard: if she couldn't replicate it across many books or authors, it wasn't worth doing. One-time wins didn't interest her.
  • Regnery became one of the rare cases where a publisher itself was a brand — readers told her they specifically bought Regnery books, which she treated as a trust obligation never to violate.
04 · The Filter
From a brand consistency standpoint, you worked with some of the most high-profile political personalities in the country. What did you learn from them about building a powerful personal brand that an entrepreneur could apply even without fame?
  • The clearest lesson from high-profile authors was that brand consistency is more valuable than any single opportunity — she watched a well-known political commentator damage their following by endorsing a candidate misaligned with their established positions.
  • She passed on books or directions that would make a loyal reader say 'I don't understand why you did that' — that confusion was the signal that a line had been crossed.
  • The filter was not about agreeing with the audience on everything, but about always coming from a place the audience expected — new or provocative ideas were fine, unexpected identity shifts were not.
  • On the publishing side, she declined entire categories rather than chasing market trends — if a book type didn't map to Regnery's defined audience, it wasn't considered regardless of commercial potential.
  • She applied the same filter to marketing tactics: anything done purely for short-term clicks or impressions that didn't connect back to the core brand was dismissed as 'cotton candy' — momentary and without value.
05 · The People
You spent two decades working with presidents, vice presidents, governors, speakers of the house, and first ladies. What did you actually learn about working with high-profile personalities, and why does that matter to how a book gets made?
  • The authors Regnery published were not primarily writers — their value was that they had something genuinely interesting and unique to say, and Marji's job was first to confirm that was true, then to make sure it came through on the page.
  • She treated every author's existing audience as a trust relationship she was responsible for protecting — if a loyal reader would say 'I don't understand why you did that,' the book or direction was off limits.
  • She coached even experienced media personalities on a distinction most of them had never considered: doing good media and doing media that actually sells books are not the same skill, and the second one has to be taught.
  • She required authors to stop pitching their book and start pitching the idea inside it — producers don't book books, they book conversations about things their audience cares about right now.
  • The gratitude she describes in her closing advice is not abstract — it shaped how she worked with people whose egos and public profiles could have made every relationship difficult, and she credits it with helping her stay useful rather than deferential.
06 · What Broke
You applied direct marketing principles to book publishing from the start. As the company scaled and you were putting out more books with more authors, what specifically stopped working that you had to rebuild?
  • The thing that broke was the assumption that a publicist pitching 'I have an author with a book' was a viable media strategy — at scale, that approach produced a wall of rejections and the feedback that outlets had stopped doing book interviews entirely.
  • The fix was structural: Marji reframed every media pitch away from the book and toward the idea, requiring publicists to answer 'why does this matter today' before they ever mentioned a title.
  • She also discovered that authors who were excellent on camera still failed to move copies because they didn't know how to create curiosity from inside an interview — being compelling and being commercial turned out to be separate problems.
  • The solution she built was a repeatable method: go through the manuscript and extract numbered lists — secrets, myths, steps, keys — then use one item from the list on air while making clear the rest live in the book.
  • The lesson was that the system that works for one or two books breaks when you're running it across dozens simultaneously — every element of the marketing process had to be teachable, transferable, and not dependent on her being in the room.
07 · Worth Knowing
Book publishing has been around for centuries. Why didn't anyone think to run it like a direct marketing business before, and what did that gap actually cost publishers who ignored it?
  • When Marji attended book publishing conferences and asked small publishers what they specialized in, the most common answer was 'a little of everything' — a response she read as proof the industry had never been forced to think about audience first.
  • Traditional publishing evaluated books on craft and editorial merit, with sales treated as a downstream problem someone else would solve — the direct marketing discipline of knowing your buyer before you make the product simply didn't exist in the culture.
  • Because no one in the industry spoke the language of testing, most publishers couldn't answer the basic direct marketing question: what decision will this test result drive? They gathered data without connecting it to action.
  • The absence of audience thinking meant most publishers couldn't answer why a gatekeeper's viewers should care about a book right now — a question that Marji treated as the single most important thing to have answered before a book went to market.
  • The gap created a real competitive advantage for Regnery: by being the one publisher that knew its audience deeply, tested deliberately, and marketed to a defined reader rather than to everyone, it became one of the rare cases where the publisher itself became a trusted brand — something the rest of the industry considered impossible.

The Misfit 3™

Marji's Three

The three things Marji would leave behind for the generations that come after.

01

Define Success First

Before you start any project, answer the question: what would make me feel successful at the end of this? Knowing what success looks like will point you in the right direction from day one.

02

Keep Doing What Works

When something works, keep doing it. Don't change for change's sake, and don't stop saying something just because you've said it before — your audience hasn't heard it, and if they did, they weren't paying attention.

03

Embrace Gratitude

We are not grateful enough. Practicing gratitude every day helps you appreciate other people, show up better for them, and stay a little humble.

Put It To Work

Five Things You Can Act On

  1. Name the problem your reader already knows they have.Don't describe the solution you're selling — describe the pain your audience feels. If they don't already know they have the problem, you'll spend all your energy convincing them before you can help them. Who is the specific person your book or product is for, and how would they describe their own problem?
  2. Pitch the idea, not the book.When reaching out to podcasts or media, never lead with 'I wrote a book.' Lead with the interesting idea inside it and tie it to something happening right now. Producers want topics their audience cares about today, not book announcements. What is the one timely angle in your work that a host's audience would care about this week?
  3. Build your list of lists before you do any media.Go through your content and pull out your seven secrets, five myths, or four steps. On any interview, share one item and tell listeners there are six more in your book. This creates curiosity and gives people a reason to buy. What numbered list could you extract from your expertise right now?
  4. Write down what you firmly believe to be true.Your beliefs are the foundation of your brand. When you know them and stay consistent with them, your audience trusts you — and trust compounds over time. Brands lose people when they do something that makes followers say, 'Wait, that doesn't sound like you.' What are the three to five beliefs you would never walk back, no matter what?
  5. Only test things you are prepared to act on.Testing is only useful if you know in advance what you will change based on the result. Too many people run tests to gather information and then do nothing differently. Before you run your next experiment, write down the single decision you will make once you have the answer.
