Episode 479 · The Misfit Entrepreneur

The Buyback Time Formula, How George Rivera Freed 60+ Hours a Week and Doubled His Income

with George Rivera — Entrepreneur, Strategic Advisor, and Founder of One Call Away

Apple Podcasts Spotify YouTube
The 60-Second Version
  • George Rivera built an eight-figure business but was working 90-hour weeks and missing his son's childhood, until a deathbed message from his father forced him to confront the cost.
  • He discovered that 50 of his 90 weekly hours were spent on tasks he could outsource for $50 an hour or less, freeing him to compress his real work to 20 highly focused hours.
  • His Buyback Time Formula walks founders through auditing their time, then eliminating, automating, or delegating tasks—and critically, delegating outcomes rather than just tasks.
  • The Two-Week Vacation Test is the benchmark for a truly freed business: if the company grows while the founder is completely off the grid, the system is working.
  • The deeper goal is intentional re-engagement with family, starting with small daily touchpoints and building to a presence children can count on, which Rivera models through his son Leo's entrepreneurial journey.
About the Guest

George Rivera

George Rivera is a 31-year entrepreneur who has scaled multiple seven- and eight-figure businesses, generating over $400 million in career sales. After nearly losing his family while running a $20 million-per-year company, he rebuilt his business around a system he calls the Buyback Time Formula. He now advises founder-dads on reclaiming 10 to 30-plus hours per week without sacrificing profit, and is the founder of the Founder Dad Tables community based in Austin, Texas.

The Conversation

What We Covered

01 · The Origin Story
You built and scaled a massive company, were successful by every measure, but where it really mattered — at home — you weren't doing that. You had a come-to-Jesus moment and changed everything. Take us through that story. How did you get to where you are now?
  • George started his first business at 17 in the mid-1990s in offline direct response — direct mail and classified ads in newspapers — then moved online a couple of years later and has stayed multi-channel ever since.
  • Over a 31-year career he scaled companies to over $400 million in combined sales, including one brand doing multiple eight figures per year by 2018.
  • Two weeks before his father died, his dad told him: "Don't miss Leo's games. I miss too many of yours." George's son Leo was nine months old at the time and George was already missing his first milestones — and couldn't even name what he had missed them for.
  • The words landed because George remembered counting only one of his father's appearances across roughly 40 high school basketball games, watching teammates get cheered on by their dads while no one cheered for him.
  • He received the message, told himself he'd change tomorrow, and then spent three more years scaling the business — printing money, hitting record numbers, and in his own words hating life — before the situation with his family became a crisis.
  • A coach and mentor talked him off the ledge of burning the business down; the mentor's single line — "Stop doing what you don't like doing and just keep doing what you're good at" — was what finally clicked after 23 years in business.
02 · The Breakthrough
A lot of founders think the answer to being overworked is just to grow more. You discovered the answer was actually to be needed less. Talk about that shift and why it's so hard for high-performing entrepreneurs to make.
  • George identified that at peak overload he was working 90 hours a week, and when he audited his time he found that 50 of those 90 hours were spent on tasks he could have outsourced for $50 an hour or less — roughly $2,500 a week for a company pushing $20 million annually.
  • The core design flaw was that he had a team to handle operations but no escalation rules — people could interrupt him at any point with any question, creating constant context-switching that destroyed productivity.
  • He built what he calls a Time Freedom Audit: get every activity out of your head and into documentation, then run each item through three filters — eliminate it, automate it, or delegate it.
  • His preferred delegation method is a Loom screen recording of how a task is done, fed into an AI tool to generate an SOP, handed to the team member with 10 to 30 minutes to review while he's present for questions — and then the owner never touches that task again.
  • The result was compressing from 90 hours down to 20 hours a week by design, while actually doubling his income, because the hours that remained were spent entirely inside his zone of genius: creating new offers and entering new marketplaces.
03 · The Growth Engine
You have a test you call the two-week vacation test. What is it, why is that the benchmark, and what does it reveal when a founder can't pass it?
  • On the first onboarding call with a new client, George has them open their calendar and block two weeks roughly six months out — the deliberate distance is what gives enough time to build and stress-test the infrastructure before the deadline arrives.
  • The path to that date runs through 90-day milestones: low-hanging-fruit tasks off the founder's plate within the first couple of weeks, a working version-one system by day 90, then a stress-testing phase from day 90 to the vacation date.
  • Absence rehearsal tests escalate in length — an afternoon off, then an overnight, then a long weekend — each one surfacing a gap the first 90 days didn't catch, which then gets patched before the next test.
  • During the actual two-week block the founder goes completely off the grid except for a daily scorecard delivered by their number two, covering five to eight key numbers — top line, bottom line, and critical metrics in between — drawn from a single dashboard rather than seven or eight separate logins.
  • Decision rights are set in advance so the team can operate without approvals: George's example is anything under $500 in refunds or tool subscriptions gets acted on without escalation, anything over comes to a structured 15-minute Thursday morning call rather than interrupting the founder ad hoc.
  • The test is considered passed only when the business continues to grow — not just hold steady — during the two weeks, proving the founder is no longer the bottleneck to the company's performance.
04 · The Filter
Working fewer hours while making more money sounds counterintuitive. How does that actually happen mechanically, and how do you decide where your remaining hours go?
  • George applies a strict zone-of-genius filter to his own calendar: the only work that reaches him is work that requires his specific skill — creating new offers and entering new marketplaces — everything else is blocked by the escalation rules and decision-rights framework already in place.
  • Tool and subscription spend is reviewed on a recurring Thursday morning call rather than approved on demand, because small recurring purchases compound quickly and need periodic pruning rather than blanket approval authority.
  • The guiding principle for what not to pursue is: if someone else can do it for $50 an hour or less, it should never reach the founder — the math on 50 such hours a week makes the delegation cost trivial relative to the revenue an eight-figure business generates per hour of focused founder attention.
  • George explicitly passed on the option of burning the business down or pursuing a fast sale — both would have solved the personal crisis but destroyed the asset; instead the filter became redesigning the business rather than exiting it.
  • For his current advisory work the filter is equally clear: he works with founder dads specifically, not founders broadly, which lets him keep the scope of One Call Away narrow and the message direct rather than trying to serve every overworked entrepreneur.
05 · The People
You rebuilt the business around systems and decision rights, but at the end of the day it still runs on people. What does the human side of that look like, and what did you get wrong about leadership before you figured it out?
  • George's original version of leadership was being the company's best firefighter — whenever a problem surfaced, he stepped in, solved it, and absorbed the dopamine hit of the team saying he was the one they could always count on.
  • He eventually recognized that was the worst possible leadership model: team members left unable to solve problems independently, and when they moved to their next job they had nothing to show for their time except "I handed it to George and he fixed it."
  • The replacement model is empowering people with both the skills and the explicit permission to own outcomes — not just tasks — so they become more valuable assets to the business now and to wherever they go next.
  • George structures team interaction through a standing 15-minute Thursday morning call rather than open-access interruptions, which gives people a predictable channel to raise questions and keeps the founder out of constant context-switching.
  • His open-dinner model at his home in Austin — where founder dads fly in, "take the mask off," and get to the root of what is actually keeping them up at night — is the core delivery mechanism for the human work inside One Call Away, and he says men regularly end up in tears once the real issues surface.
06 · What Broke
You had a team in place at your eight-figure company but you were still working 90-hour weeks. What specifically had broken, and what did you build to replace it?
  • The thing that broke was escalation protocol — or rather, the complete absence of one. George had operators for every function but no rules governing when or how those operators could interrupt him, so the team treated him as an always-available answer machine.
  • The result was constant context-switching throughout the day: any team member could surface any question at any moment, destroying the deep focus blocks that high-value creative work requires.
  • The replacement was a structured decision-rights framework: a clear dollar threshold below which team members act without approval, a defined weekly call window for anything that does need discussion, and a requirement that anyone escalating a problem must first document what they tried and what they suggest.
  • Alongside decision rights, George introduced absence rehearsal tests — progressively longer periods of deliberate unavailability — each one exposing a gap the initial system design had missed, which then got patched before the next test.
  • The single scorecard replacing seven or eight separate logins was the operational manifestation of the fix: five to eight key numbers delivered by his number two gave him full situational awareness without requiring him to be inside the business daily.
07 · Worth Knowing
You spent 31 years in direct response before stepping out publicly as a personal brand. What does a founder actually have to solve when they make that transition, and why is it harder than it looks?
  • George built over $400 million in combined sales operating almost entirely behind the scenes in direct response — a world where the offer and the channel do the persuading and the founder's identity is largely irrelevant to the customer.
  • When he decided to carry the message of One Call Away publicly, he ran into a cold structural problem: the direct response playbook does not transfer to personal branding, and the market's first question was simply "who are you and why should we believe you?"
  • The skills that made him exceptional in one category — multi-channel offer creation, media buying logic, conversion optimization — gave him no credibility runway in a category where trust is built through visible presence over time.
  • His answer was to treat the personal brand launch the same way he would treat entering any new marketplace: podcasts, masterminds, stages, and a book, each channel building proof that compounds rather than a single campaign that either works or doesn't.
  • The deeper lesson is that domain expertise and category authority are not the same asset — a founder can be genuinely world-class at building businesses and start at zero when the product being sold is the founder themselves, which means the transition requires patience that pure performance marketers are not wired for.

Put It To Work

Five Things You Can Act On

  1. Do a Time Freedom Audit This Week.List every task you did in the last seven days. Flag anything you could hand off for $50 an hour or less. George found 50 of his 90 weekly hours sitting in that bucket — that is not a time problem, that is a delegation problem. How many of your hours belong to someone else?
  2. Stop Delegating Tasks — Delegate Outcomes.Record a Loom of how the task gets done, drop the transcript into AI, and turn it into an SOP. Hand it off with a clear picture of what success looks like, then get out of the way. If you keep owning the task, you stay the bottleneck. Who on your team could own an outcome you are still carrying?
  3. Block Your Two-Week Vacation Test Right Now.Open your calendar and block two weeks six months out. Then work backward — stress test with an afternoon off, then an overnight, then a long weekend. Each absence surfaces what still breaks without you, and gives you a specific thing to fix. What would have to be true about your business for you to disappear for two weeks and come back to growth?
  4. Set Decision Rights Before You Hand Off Power.Define a dollar threshold below which your team can act without you — for purchases, refunds, tool subscriptions. Review those decisions on a fixed cadence, not in real time. Empowerment without accountability is just chaos with a better name. What decisions are still landing on your desk that your team should own?
  5. Pick One of Three Daily Touch Points With Your Family.Morning wake-up, after school, or the window between dinner and bedtime — pick one and show up for it consistently this week. It does not have to be long. The goal is to become reliable enough that your family starts counting on you again. Which of those three moments have you been missing most?
