The Conversation

What We Covered

01 · The Origin Story
You started at such a young age. Take five minutes or so and tell us how you got to where you're at.
  • Mike's grandmother, Granny Elizabeth, started selling collard sandwiches — collard greens pressed between two fried pieces of cornbread — out of desperation after her husband of 50 years developed a back injury that turned out to be cancer and died, leaving the family without its primary breadwinner.
  • At 11 years old, Mike helped count and store the cash: coins and bills went into Folger's coffee cans, and large bills were wrapped in tinfoil labeled 'liver' and hidden in the freezer so that any would-be thief would skip right past them.
  • That sandwich business, run out of a 10-by-70 yellow trailer in Laurel Hill, North Carolina, gave Mike his first experience with money as survival — and planted the idea that helping people with money was what he was meant to do.
  • The path into finance started at the bank branch where Mike and his grandmother opened their first account. Mike liked the branch manager's suit, learned his route — University of North Carolina Chapel Hill, then up through the banks — and decided to copy it.
  • After college, Mike spent roughly 10 years working inside banks, where he saw firsthand that profit outranked the customer. He left to go independent, picked up his CFP designation, began teaching at Old Dominion University, and eventually built One Oak Financial around the idea that every person's financial plan should be as one of a kind as the person it serves.
02 · The Breakthrough
What is broken in the traditional financial planning industry, and how do you disrupt it with the way you do things?
  • Mike identified a core structural problem: large institutions — Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan Chase, BlackRock, MassMutual — report record profits every quarter, and those profits come from using customer money, regardless of whether the customer is an ordinary saver or Elon Musk.
  • The industry's defense mechanism is complexity. Products like private placements, private credit, mutual funds, and index funds are explained in ways that overwhelm people, triggering what Mike calls the paralysis penalty — the brain defaults to safety, people park money in savings accounts, and the institutions keep winning.
  • Mike's counter-model sequences the relationship differently: educate first, coach second, plan third. Only after a client understands their situation and can articulate why money matters to them does a financial plan get built.
  • The plan is anchored to two things the client defines before any numbers are discussed: their 'why' — why money is important to them — and their vision for how they want to live, including health, community, and impact.
  • The firm's name encodes the philosophy: One Oak stands for 'one of a kind,' signaling that cookie-cutter solutions designed to feed institutional profit margins are the problem, not the answer.
03 · The Growth Engine
Walk us through the six things people can do today to improve their financial picture and legally reduce their taxes — especially for business owners.
  • The first lever is entity structure. Sole proprietors earning $100,000 or more are leaving money on the table by filing 1099 income on a personal return; an LLC with an S-Corp election limits Social Security and FICA taxes to actual salary rather than total earnings, and a C-Corp becomes relevant at the top 37% bracket or when a future sale is planned, since QSBS exempts the first $10 million in gains on a qualifying C-Corp held five or more years.
  • Mainstream code deductions — the Augusta Rule (renting your home to your business for up to 14 days tax-free at market rates), Section 179 equipment expensing (Mike used it to have his company purchase a Mercedes GLE 350, making the full cost a pre-tax business expense), and the family office structure — make up the second and third levers.
  • Hiring minor children through the business is a fourth lever: paying a child up to roughly $16,000–$19,000 per year as a 1099 for legitimate services creates a business deduction, and the income often clears the standard deduction threshold tax-free at the child's level, with no Social Security obligation for minors under certain structures.
  • A solo 401(k) or similar retirement account is the fifth lever for high-income self-employed earners, sheltering income from current-year taxation while building long-term assets.
  • Geographic arbitrage — Act 60 in Puerto Rico — is the sixth and most aggressive lever. Mike relocated his personal residence and registered his firm under the Export Services decree, which requires physical presence on the island, demonstrating closer connection than anywhere else, and donating $10,000 per year to Puerto Rican charities. In return, the business pays 2% corporate tax and Mike withdraws profits as tax-free dividends — legally, as a U.S. citizen in a U.S. territory.
  • Mike described building toward four Inc. 5000-recognized businesses simultaneously, and while developing a franchising model for One Oak Financial he launched a coaching program to teach other advisors his methodology — shortening the runway from entry to success and extending his firm's reach to clients he would never serve directly.
04 · The Filter
You've said entrepreneurs often just build themselves a glorified job. How does someone know which one they have, and how do they make the shift to owning a real business?
  • Mike's stated goal — four businesses on the Inc. 5000 list within three years — functions as the filter itself: any opportunity that requires him to remain the sole operator disqualifies itself, because that goal is mathematically impossible if he owns a job rather than an asset.
  • The test for whether something is a business or a job is whether other people can generate revenue and make decisions independent of the founder. If the answer is no, it stays off the list.
  • He passed on the ambition to put One Oak Financial on a building in every major city — a Charles Schwab or Fidelity scale national footprint — after concluding that private equity dynamics and regulatory structure make that path inaccessible to a founding entrepreneur today.
  • The franchising model replaced the national-branch ambition as the preferred expansion path, keeping capital requirements and operational complexity lower while still extending geographic reach.
  • Mike applies a 'Mount Rushmore' decision filter when evaluating direction: he stress-tests choices against the lens of Jeff Bezos's long-horizon thinking, Charlie Munger's investing discipline, Darren Hardy's compounding principles, and his grandmother's example of selling a premium product on the strength of story and human connection rather than competing on price.
05 · The People
You coach other advisors and you're building toward a franchise model. What does the human side of One Oak Financial actually look like — the people you hire, the people you serve, and why that matters to how the business runs?
  • Mike's firm employs people across five countries, which means the day-to-day operation already runs independent of any single location — a structural requirement given that Mike himself is based in Puerto Rico while clients are on the mainland.
  • The client relationship is sequenced deliberately: educate first, coach second, plan third — so the first thing a client experiences is someone trying to understand them, not sell them a product.
  • Before any numbers are discussed, every client is asked two questions: why is money important to you, and what does your vision for living well actually look like? The answers to those questions, not asset levels, determine what the plan contains.
  • Mike's coaching program for other financial advisors exists partly because it extends the firm's reach to clients he will never personally serve — advisors who learn his methodology carry it to their own books of business.
  • The business is B Corp certified, meaning impact over profit is a formal structural commitment, not a marketing line — it shapes who gets hired and how the firm positions itself against institutions that report record earnings every quarter.
06 · What Broke
You said owning a business is something you had to learn through failure. What specifically broke, and what did you replace it with?
  • The original ambition was a national brick-and-mortar footprint — One Oak Financial on a building in every major city, a name as recognizable as Charles Schwab or Fidelity.
  • Private equity dynamics and financial regulation make that path effectively closed to a founding entrepreneur today — the capital requirements and compliance structure are built for institutions, not independents trying to scale from scratch.
  • Mike abandoned the national-branch model after concluding it was structurally inaccessible, not just difficult — a distinction that matters because the lesson is about reading the actual market, not about working harder.
  • The franchising model replaced it: lower capital requirements, lower operational complexity, geographic reach without the regulatory ceiling that stopped the branch-office plan.
  • While the franchise infrastructure was being built out by attorneys and accountants, Mike launched the advisor coaching program to fill the gap — turning the waiting period into a revenue stream and a proof-of-concept for the methodology he planned to franchise.
07 · Worth Knowing
Why is the financial planning industry structurally set up so that an independent founder can't build a national firm the way Schwab or Fidelity did?
  • The registered investment advisor and broker-dealer regulatory framework was built around institutions with compliance departments, not solo or small-firm founders — the cost and complexity of multi-state licensing, custody arrangements, and regulatory examination scales against small operators.
  • Private equity has moved aggressively into the RIA space, acquiring independent firms and rolling them up, which has compressed the window for organic national growth — by the time an independent firm is large enough to consider expansion, acquisition offers arrive before the infrastructure does.
  • This means the category of 'nationally recognized independent financial planning firm built by its founder from scratch' is largely empty today — the firms that hold that position were built in a regulatory and competitive environment that no longer exists.
  • Mike's conclusion was not that the ambition was wrong but that the vehicle — company-owned branches — was the wrong structure for the current market; franchising transfers the compliance and operational burden to individual franchisees while preserving the brand and methodology.
  • The practical implication for any advisor or service-business founder watching this space: the path to national scale now runs through licensing your system to other operators, not through opening locations yourself — the economics and the regulations have permanently changed who can afford to grow the old way.
