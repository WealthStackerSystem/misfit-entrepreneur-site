From Ivory Tower to Startup: How Pardis Mahdavi Is Rebuilding Education for the AI Economy

with Pardis Mahdavi — Founder, My Uni

Apple Podcasts Spotify YouTube
The 60-Second Version
  • Mahdavi spent 25 years inside elite academic institutions and watched firsthand as rising costs, a 43-million-person dropout crisis, and a widening skills gap made higher ed increasingly irrelevant to employers.
  • The passage of Workforce Pell in 2025 is, in her view, the biggest shift in higher education since the GI Bill, tying federal dollars to credentials that demonstrably connect to workforce outcomes.
  • Her company My Uni and its software platform L5 reverse-engineer education by starting with what employers need, then building accredited credentials backward from those requirements rather than forward from traditional curricula.
  • She argues that AI should free humans to slow down and do deeper thinking, not simply accelerate output, and that every credential should bake in deliberate thinking skills alongside technical ones.
  • Her personal pivot from institution leader to startup founder came when she stopped suppressing her curiosity and discomfort with broken systems and started making decisions from what she calls a beautiful inner state rather than a suffering one.
About the Guest

Pardis Mahdavi

Dr. Pardis Mahdavi is an anthropologist, nine-time author, and former university president, provost, and dean at institutions including Arizona State University and the University of Denver. After 25 years at the top of academia, she founded My Uni and the workforce software platform L5 to build employer-driven credentials for the AI economy. She sits on the board of the Lumina Foundation, is a lifetime member of the Council on Foreign Relations, and has advised the UN, Google, and the US government.

The Conversation

What We Covered

01 · The Origin Story
You spent roughly 25 years at the very top of the academic establishment — university president, provost, dean. You then turned around and started disrupting the same system you helped run. How did you get from there to here?
  • Pardis trained as an anthropologist, which gave her a specific lens: she was taught to watch how culture moves and what institutions need to do to keep up with it.
  • While running universities, she used that anthropological background to see what colleagues around her were not naming — the institutions were failing students on one side and failing workforce needs on the other.
  • The breaking point came from a conversation inside a YPO group of CEOs who told her directly that graduates they hired still weren't ready to do the job — the employers had to finish training people who had already taken on debt for a degree.
  • She realized she had been suppressing the traits that defined her — curiosity, creativity, challenging the status quo — to fit inside institutions that couldn't accommodate them, and that suppression had made her worse at her job and miserable in the process.
  • Rather than wait for the institution to change, she stepped down and built a company specifically because she had sat in the chair of the people she now sells to, and knew exactly what they were struggling with.
02 · The Breakthrough
Tell us more about what you're building with My Uni. The idea seems to be jobs first, education built around them — employers define the skills, you build the credential pathways, and because of the technology you can do it fast. How does that actually work, and how would a university engage with it?
  • The core insight was to reverse-engineer education: start with what workforce has clearly stated it needs, then build credentials backward from that demand rather than forward from academic tradition.
  • The company built a software product called L5, a workforce health platform, with a sub-tool called F3 — a faculty facilitator and course-builder companion — that runs a university's existing courses through an algorithm and returns a red, yellow, or green rating for each one.
  • Green courses are already eligible for Workforce Pell dollars with no changes; yellow courses are close and the software identifies the five specific things that would qualify them; red courses require more substantial rebuilding.
  • The passage of Workforce Pell under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act — which Pardis calls the biggest change in higher education since the GI Bill — created the external funding pressure that made universities willing to act, tying government dollars directly to credentials with demonstrable workforce relevance.
  • State-by-state labor demand data feeds into the platform, enabling predictive analytics: Arizona needs a specific number of microchip engineers for TSMC, Illinois is leading in quantum, Missouri needs AI-trained workers — and the software helps institutions align their offerings to that specific, localized demand.
03 · The Growth Engine
You went from running institutions with established infrastructure to founding a startup — raising capital, building teams across time zones, shipping software. What does the operation actually look like now, and how are you scaling it?
  • The team operates across three continents, with her CTO based in Australia — a distribution model made possible entirely by the same technology stack the company sells.
  • The client base is universities and colleges themselves, institutions that already know their model is broken but don't know what to do about it — the pitch is not ideological but practical: here is the tool, here is the money it unlocks, here is exactly how to use it.
  • Workforce Pell, effective July of this year, functions as a built-in market-creation mechanism — the government pot of money designated for workforce-relevant credentials is growing, which means every institution that hasn't yet aligned its offerings has a financial incentive to become a customer.
  • Pardis leverages her 26 years inside academia not just as credibility but as a navigation advantage — she knows how difficult institutional change is from the inside, and the product is specifically designed to lower the activation energy for people who have been teaching the same course for 30 years.
04 · The Filter
You're sitting at this intersection of a massive broken system, new government funding, AI disruption, and K-12 reform all happening at once. How do you decide what My Uni actually pursues versus what you leave alone?
  • Pardis is explicit that she is not trying to replace the Harvards, Columbias, and Stanfords — those institutions have multi-billion-dollar endowments and will survive regardless; the focus is on where most Americans actually attend and where most Americans actually drop out.
  • The company does not pursue every credential type — the filter is workforce relevance as defined by the Workforce Pell framework, which means credentials that can be tied to specific, demonstrable job outcomes in sectors where state-level labor data shows measurable demand.
  • She draws a deliberate line between the technical credential side of the business and the broader cultural conversation about AI and human development — the latter informs the product philosophy but is not the product itself.
  • K-12 is acknowledged as a parallel problem she tracks closely as a parent and researcher, but My Uni's current scope stops at higher education; she names the Alpha school model as an example of that space already experimenting on its own without her needing to enter it.
  • Decisions about what to build are run through an internal test she describes as checking which inner state they come from — she has observed that every decision made from fear or panic has not worked out, while decisions made from curiosity and expansion have, making that distinction a functional filter on company direction.
05 · The People
You built My Uni specifically because you had sat inside the institutions you now sell to. What does that insider knowledge tell you about the people on both sides of this problem — the university staff trying to change and the students the system is failing?
  • The faculty resistance Pardis anticipated was not laziness — it was the same paralysis she witnessed when institutions tried to move courses online: people saying 'I've been teaching this class for 30 years' until a forcing event, like the pandemic, removed the choice entirely.
  • Students who dropped out were not failures; they were often the ones being headhunted mid-degree by employers, only to discover their employer still had to finish training them anyway — meaning the credential was not certifying readiness, it was certifying time served.
  • The YPO CEOs she was in community with were the ones who told her plainly that graduates weren't work-ready on arrival — that candid employer voice is now baked into how My Uni defines what a credential has to demonstrate before it qualifies.
  • Pardis describes her university clients as institutions that already know their model is broken but don't know what to do — the product is designed specifically for that person: overwhelmed, overworked, not ideologically resistant, just without a clear next step.
  • Her own experience of suppressing curiosity and creativity to fit institutional norms made her a worse leader — she treats that personal cost as a data point about what happens when people can't work from their actual strengths, and it shapes how she thinks about what workforce-ready really means.
06 · What Broke
Trust in higher education has been declining for years. What specifically broke inside the institution that made the system stop working, and what has to replace it?
  • What broke was the narrative link between a credential and a job outcome — for decades, a degree implied workforce readiness, and that implication held even as employers quietly built internal training programs to finish what universities started.
  • The public secret, as Pardis names it, is that everyone inside the system knew the bridge between learning and earning was broken, but the institutional incentive to name it out loud did not exist until money was tied to the outcome.
  • LinkedIn Learning certificates and other trust-migration alternatives emerged to fill the gap, but employers pushed back — a certificate from a non-accredited source didn't tell them whether someone could actually do the job, so the alternative credentialing market solved awareness without solving verification.
  • Workforce Pell is what replaced the broken incentive structure: by tying government dollars to credentials with demonstrable workforce relevance, it created external pressure strong enough to move institutions that internal reform arguments had failed to move for years.
  • The specific mechanism My Uni built to replace the old course-design process is the F3 faculty facilitator tool — it removes the burden of figuring out what to change by telling faculty exactly which five things would qualify a yellow-rated course, lowering the activation energy enough that a 30-year veteran can act without starting over.
07 · Worth Knowing
You said trust doesn't die when institutions fail — it migrates. What does that actually mean, and why does it matter for anyone trying to build something in a space where the dominant institution is losing credibility?
  • Pardis's research on underground networks in Iran gave her a framework she later applied directly to higher education: when a dominant institution loses legitimacy, the trust doesn't disappear — it relocates to whatever fills the vacuum, and tracking where it goes reveals where the real market is forming.
  • In Iran, government failure drove people underground to organize their own social infrastructure; in higher education, the same dynamic produced mastermind communities, LinkedIn Learning, and employer-run internal training programs — all attempts to capture trust that had left the university.
  • The critical insight from her research is that polarization of trust is more dangerous than a simple decline in trust — across political lines, Americans now diverge sharply on how much they trust the same institutions, which means a single solution cannot assume a shared baseline of credibility.
  • For anyone building in a space disrupted by institutional failure, Pardis's framework suggests the question is not 'how do I compete with the incumbent' but 'where has the trust already migrated, and what does that population actually need that the incumbent stopped providing' — in My Uni's case, the answer was rigorous, verifiable credentials that employer networks would actually recognize.
  • The window to capture migrated trust is narrow: once people find a substitute that works well enough, they stop looking — which is why Workforce Pell arriving now, rather than five years from now, matters; the government funding mechanism and the trust vacuum opened at the same moment, and that alignment is what makes the market real rather than theoretical.

Pardis's Three

The three things Pardis would leave behind for the generations that come after.

01

Name the public secrets

Ask yourself what broken systems you're silently upholding. Speak up about them — more people are in on the secret than you think, and they're waiting for someone to say it out loud.

02

Check your inner state before deciding

Ask yourself whether you're making decisions from fear, panic, or feeling less than — or from curiosity, joy, and expansion. Then look back: how have your suffering-state decisions worked out versus your beautiful-state ones?

03

Turn outward when things collapse

When nothing makes sense and everything feels like it's falling apart, turn to community and do something for someone else. That is the fastest way to start turning things around for yourself.

Put It To Work

Five Things You Can Act On

  1. Reverse-engineer your offer from what the market actually needs.Pardis built My Uni by starting with what employers said they needed and working backward to build the credential. Most institutions — and most businesses — do the opposite and wonder why no one buys. What would you build if you started with the customer's outcome instead of your own expertise?
  2. Stop defending a broken system just because you built it.Pardis spent 25 years inside higher ed before admitting publicly that it was failing students. The longer you protect something that isn't working, the more it costs the people who depend on you. What system in your business are you quietly defending that you already know is broken?
  3. Audit which inner state is driving your biggest decisions.Fear and panic produce different outcomes than curiosity and gratitude — and you already have the data if you look back at your track record. Before your next major decision, name the emotion underneath it. Are you acting from expansion or from scarcity?
  4. Get ahead of the Workforce Pell shift now.The government just tied a growing pool of federal dollars to credentials that demonstrably connect to jobs — Pardis calls it the biggest change in higher ed since the GI Bill. If you're in education, workforce development, or corporate training, this is a funding runway most people don't know exists yet. Have you looked at whether your programs qualify?
  5. Let the things that make you a misfit be your competitive advantage.Pardis spent years suppressing her curiosity and truth-telling inside institutions that punished it, and it made her worse at her job. The moment she built something around those traits, everything changed. What quality have you been told to dial back that might actually be your edge?
