What You Actually Want

July 28, 2026

You have been meaning to figure out what you actually want for years. You know this because the thought surfaces every January, every birthday, every time a quarter ends and you look at the scoreboard and feel a low-grade dissatisfaction you cannot quite name. Then something urgent comes up and the question goes back in the drawer.

That is the real problem. Not laziness. Not lack of ambition. Most people never get what they want because they never get specific enough about what that is. They stay at the surface level — more money, more freedom, more time — and surface-level answers produce surface-level results.

The fix is not complicated. It requires a few hours, a quiet room, and a willingness to keep asking past the first answer that sounds good.

Start With the First Answer, Then Reject It

Ask yourself: what do I want? Write down whatever comes out. Then ask why you want that. Then ask why that matters. Then ask what life actually looks like if you get it. Four levels deep, minimum. Most people stop at level one because the first answer feels satisfying enough to be real. It is not. It is a socially acceptable placeholder.

You say you want financial freedom. Why? So you can stop worrying. Why does worry bother you? Because it makes you feel out of control. What would being in control actually look like on a Tuesday morning? Now you are getting somewhere. Now there is something specific enough to build toward.

The surface answer is almost never the true answer. It is the answer you have rehearsed.

Separate What You Want From What You Think You Should Want

This is where the work gets uncomfortable. A lot of the goals people carry around belong to someone else — a parent, a peer, an earlier version of themselves who made a decision twenty years ago and never revisited it. You have to be willing to put those down.

I talked about this kind of reckoning in episode 290, reflecting on what real commitment requires. You cannot be committed to something you do not actually want. Commitment to a borrowed goal is just obligation wearing ambition's clothes. It drains you instead of fueling you, and eventually it stops working entirely.

Ask yourself: if no one who knew me would ever find out, would I still want this? If the answer is no, that goal is not yours.

Make It Concrete Enough to Be Wrong

Vague goals cannot fail, which means they cannot succeed either. "I want to be healthier" is not a goal. "I want to run a half-marathon by October 15th" is a goal. The specificity is not about pressure. It is about honesty. A vague goal is a way of wanting something without committing to it.

Once you have gone four levels deep and confirmed the want is actually yours, write down what achieving it looks like in precise terms. What do you have? What do you do on a typical day? What does your bank account say? Who is around you? Precision is not rigidity. It is clarity, and clarity is what separates people who move from people who drift.

People don't rise to the level of their expectations. They rise to the level they prepare for. And you cannot prepare for something you have not defined.

Schedule the Hours, or It Does Not Happen

None of this works if it stays theoretical. Block two to three hours on your calendar this week. Not a lunch. Not a gap between calls. A real block, treated like the most important meeting of the quarter — because it is. Go somewhere without your phone. Bring paper. Write the question at the top: what do I want? Then keep asking why until the answer changes into something you have never said out loud before.

That moment when the answer surprises you is the moment you are finally talking to yourself honestly. Start there.
