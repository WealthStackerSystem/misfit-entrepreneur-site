What 9/11 Still Teaches Entrepreneurs

September 7, 2026

The towers fell on a Tuesday morning. People who had been strangers on a Monday were pulling each other out of rubble by noon. No memo went out. No committee formed. No one waited to be told what mattered.

Twenty-five years later, we are far enough from that day that some people have started to treat it like history. A chapter. Something to acknowledge and move past. I think that is a mistake — especially for entrepreneurs, who are supposed to be the ones who remember what things are actually made of.

We are the backbone of this country. Not a talking point. A structural fact. Small businesses employ more Americans than any other engine. Entrepreneurs are the ones who build the communities, fund the little leagues, hire the neighbors, and absorb the shocks when things go wrong. What we believe about how to conduct ourselves matters beyond our own bottom lines.

So here are three principles that were on full display that September — not as inspiration, but as a standard worth holding.

1. Ordinary people do extraordinary things when they know what they stand for.

The passengers on Flight 93 had twenty minutes and incomplete information. They took a vote, made a decision, and acted. Clarity of purpose does not require certainty of outcome. It requires knowing what you will not let happen on your watch. Most entrepreneurs I respect operate exactly this way — not because they always know what comes next, but because they are clear on what they are unwilling to compromise.

I talked about the foundations of this kind of principled conviction in episode 405. Desire grounded in principle moves differently than desire grounded in appetite. One builds. The other just accumulates.

2. Unity is a decision, not a feeling.

In the weeks after 9/11, people were not unified because things were going well. They were unified because they chose to be, in the face of something that could have pulled them apart. The businesses, fire departments, and volunteers that showed up did not wait until conditions were right. They showed up because someone had to and they decided that someone was them.

The entrepreneurs who build lasting organizations do not wait for culture to emerge organically. They choose it, especially when it is inconvenient. Especially in the hard stretch.

3. Resilience is built before the crisis, not during it.

New York did not recover because it improvised well on September 12th. It recovered because decades of people had built institutions, relationships, and habits that held when everything was shaken. The restaurants that stayed open. The small shops that kept their people employed. The contractors who showed up for months and asked nothing. That is not heroism. That is character accumulated over time, expressed under pressure.

You do not become resilient in a crisis. You find out whether you already are. In episode 340, I talked about grounding yourself in consistent truths rather than whatever narrative feels convenient right now. That practice — done quietly, repeatedly, before anything is on fire — is what makes you someone your team can count on when something actually is.

We are far enough from that Tuesday that the images have faded for a lot of people. Close enough that those of us who were adults that day still feel it somewhere in our chests when we let ourselves go back there.

Do not let the distance become amnesia. The principles those people demonstrated were not special to that moment. They are available to you every day you decide to run your business — and your life — like someone who has not forgotten what things are actually made of.
