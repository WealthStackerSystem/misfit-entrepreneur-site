Stop Manufacturing Problems When Things Are Working

August 3, 2026

You hit a stretch where everything is humming. The team is executing. The numbers are moving. Problems that used to keep you up at night have been solved. And then something shifts in you — not because the business needs it, but because stillness feels dangerous.

So you call a meeting. You reopen a process that was working fine. You layer a new initiative on top of a team that hasn't finished the last one. You manufacture urgency because momentum feels like it requires noise.

That instinct will cost you more than you realize.

There is a version of drive that builds things. And there is a version that just keeps moving because stopping feels like failing. The second one looks identical from the outside — long hours, full calendars, constant activity — but it produces something different. It produces a team that learns to distrust calm. A team that waits for the next thing to be broken open, because that is the pattern you have trained them to expect.

Dov Baron talked about this in episode 120 — the idea that real leadership operates from values, not anxiety. The leaders who manufacture problems aren't doing it for the business. They are doing it for themselves, to scratch the itch that stillness creates.

Perfection is not a destination. It is a habit of avoidance dressed up as ambition.

Here is what actually happens when a leader can't let good be good: the team stops trusting their own judgment. They solve something, look up for acknowledgment, and instead get handed the next fire. Over time, they stop feeling the satisfaction of finishing anything. They run faster and feel less. That is not a performance problem. That is a leadership problem.

The hard discipline is learning to protect what is working. Not because you have gone soft. Because you understand that momentum is fragile and people are not machines.

This doesn't mean there isn't real work to do. There is always real work. Markets shift, customers leave, competitors move. You don't have to invent the wolf — it will show up. Your job is to make sure your team has the energy to fight it when it does.

Scott Moorehead and Ryan McCarty made the case in episode 103 that culture is not a perk you bolt on — it is a structural decision that compounds over time. A team that has been chronically over-pressured does not suddenly become resilient when a real crisis hits. They have already spent that resilience on problems you invented.

You built something. It is working. The most important thing you can do right now is not wreck it by needing it to always be broken so you can feel useful.

Activity is not the same as progress. Your team knows the difference, even when you don't.

Take the win. Protect the energy. Save the urgency for when the business actually earns it.
